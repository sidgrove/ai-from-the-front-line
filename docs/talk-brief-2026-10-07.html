The talk

A copilot. Never the pilot.

A probabilistic text generator cannot be guaranteed to get the same ledger entry right every time. So it builds the tool. It does not run the close.Your thesis, from the talk brief in Sidgrove Intelligence (469 facts, 36 entries, built 6 October).

  • 1

    It is architecture, not polish. Deterministic work means the same input gives the same output every time. An LLM is probabilistic, so mistakes are certain however good the average.

  • 2

    Benchmarks are not the job. Even the Federal Reserve concedes that strong benchmark scores do not mean on-the-job tasks get completed.

  • 3

    Audit needs an explanation. Researchers who build these systems say they understand very little about how they work. An auditor cannot sign off a process nobody can explain.

  • 4

    The supplier economics are fragile. Today's prices are subsidised. Do not wire your month end to a price that may not last.

  • 5

    So: rules do the bulk, GenAI builds the rules, AI flags exceptions, a human signs.

Panel

The end of accountants? No. The end of some of the typing.

It will not replace us, not as claimed anyway. It is being oversold. The accountant who uses it as a copilot will out-work the one who does not.

  • 1

    Concede first. Usage is near universal and it saves real time. White collar jobs rose by about three million over three years, which the sceptics' own essay admits.

  • 2

    Then separate helper from engine. Using it to draft, explain and build is one thing. Trusting it as the system of record is another.

  • 3

    The headlines are forecasts by sellers. "Human-level professional work in 12 to 18 months" came from Microsoft's AI chief in February. It is a forecast, not evidence.

  • 4

    The profession's value is the signature. Judgement, accountability and an audit trail are what a client pays for, and what a model cannot give.

What the room will ask

98% of firms already use AI. Isn't that the end of the argument?

Use is not the same as running the books on it. That figure is a vendor survey of assistive use.

Vendors say they already close books with agents.

Those are vendor claims with no published error rates. They also say they escalate on low confidence, which concedes the point.

Won't the models just get good enough?

Better on average, yes. Guaranteed on every run, no. That is what a ledger needs.

Isn't this just a bubble argument?

No. The economics are context for price and continuity risk. The core case is architectural.

Should juniors be worried?

The work changes. Someone still has to know what right looks like, and you only learn that by doing it.

Panel

Buy versus Build. Build the glue. Buy the engine.

I build the layer that connects my tools and encodes how my practice works. I buy anything a vendor already does well. And I never build the ledger.

Build it

  • Internal tools and prototypes
  • Your own workflow between tools you already pay for (Xero, Sheets, payroll, Slack)
  • Reports and dashboards from data you already hold
  • Slides, simple websites, brand assets

Buy it

  • If an app already does it
  • The ledger, payroll engine, tax filing: anything regulated
  • Anything you could not support if you were ill for a month

Be honest about

  • Security. Get an engineer if it holds sensitive data
  • Rabbit holes. Do not overbuild
  • You now own the maintenance
  • The tool that builds it runs at a subsidised price

What the room will ask

Isn't building your own software exactly the risk you warn about?

The model writes the code once. The code then runs the same way every time and I can test it. That is the opposite of letting the model run the process.

What happens when you are not there?

Fair. That is the real cost of build. It is documented, it is in version control, and the ledger is still Xero.

Could a normal practice do this?

Start with one painful spreadsheet workflow. Describe it, let it build it, check the numbers against the old way. Do not start with a platform.

Why not wait for the vendors to ship it?

Some will, and then I will buy it. But the vendor roadmap is putting the model in the pilot's seat, which is the bit I do not want.

Push-back, and the one-line answer

"Adoption is near universal and saves an hour a day."

Vendor-reported, and about assistive use. It says nothing about reliability as a runtime.

"Tax tools cut prep time by 85%."

A vendor claim. Time saved drafting is not the same as trustworthy execution.

"Losses are just investment in training."

Possibly. But the price I pay today is not the price that covers the cost, and I plan on that.

"You are just quoting one sceptic."

Mostly, yes, and I say so. The figures I lean on trace back to audited accounts, the FT, Goldman, Bain and the Fed.

How far to trust this page

Most of the economic figures come from one commentator, Ed Zitron, and several are his own estimates. They show supplier fragility, not that the architecture fails on accounting tasks, so lead with the architecture and use the money as context. The "New today" figures came through a machine summary and are the ones to verify. The build-log figures were relayed by the session that did the work and have not been independently checked. One short quotation only, if you want his words: "you can't guarantee that they'll get something right", from his 2 October piece.
